The Lighthouse and the Sea

For thirty years the keeper had lived beside the sea, and the sea had never once
been quiet. On calm nights the sea whispered against the rocks like a patient
friend; on wild nights the sea rose up and struck the tower, and the keeper
climbed the stairs to keep the light burning.

The light was the whole point of the tower. The light warned every passing ship
away from the rocks, and the keeper tended the light as if it were a living
thing. He cleaned the glass each morning, trimmed the wick at dusk, and wrote in
his log the hour when the light was lit. Sailors trusted the light. They said
that no ship had ever been lost within sight of that light, and the keeper took
quiet pride in the light.

Winter came early that year. The wind turned cold, the sea turned grey, and the
lights of distant ships grew few. One evening a storm rolled in from the west,
bringing rain and thunder and a wind that shook the tower to its stones. The
keeper lit the light and watched the sea climb the rocks. The waves were higher
than he had ever seen, and for the first time in years he was afraid.

Somewhere in the dark a ship was fighting the storm. The keeper could not see
the ship, but he could imagine it: the deck awash, the sails torn, the captain
straining to find a light that was not there. He fed the lamp more oil and kept
the light steady, though the storm pressed against the glass and the tower
 groaned like an old man.

Then, near midnight, the wind dropped. The rain softened to a drizzle, the sea
fell back, and the keeper heard a sound he knew: the low, steady beat of a ship
passing safely by. In the morning he found a small boat pulled up on the beach
below the tower, and three sailors asleep in it, exhausted but alive. They had
followed the light through the storm, they said, and the light had carried them
home.

The keeper wrote the night in his log, as he wrote every night. Then he climbed
the stairs, cleaned the glass, and trimmed the wick, because the light had to
burn again that evening, and the sea, though quiet now, was never truly calm.
